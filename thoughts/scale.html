Scale - do economies of scale or emergent properties really exist?

Introduction

There are a number of phrases that suggest humans appreciate a universal principle of "conservation", even for purely social ideas:

  • Newton's 3rd Law - Action/Reaction
  • Justice - An eye for an eye
  • Making change - Can't make an omelette without breaking a few eggs
  • Something from nothing - No such thing as a free lunch

This idea of conservation, of balance, of justice, of consequence reflect the world humans occupy where this law governs much of our lives:

  • Want to drive your car? You will burn fuel.
  • Climbed to the top of the hill? To return home you must descend the hill.
  • Want to buy this item? You must pay for it.
  • Poured tea from a teapot? The teapot must now be emptier by the same volume of tea.
  • Keeping the inside of your fridge cool? The heat must now exit the fridge.
  • Moved sand from this spot on the beach? The beach now has a hole where the sand used to be.

I realise this is hammering the point home, but I want to make it clear that as humans, we are so tightly bound by this physical conservation that we go about our lives psychologically expecting this law to work all the time.

So where's the problem?

Ever since reading a book called Scale I have been repeatedly reminded of two principles which seem to come up (and sometimes cause trouble) all over the place in our news, our politics, our workplaces and our homes:

  • Economies of scale
  • Emergent porperties

Both are used in my opinion at best incorrectly, or at worst deliberately to excuse a decision.

Economies of scale, for example, can be used by managerial classes to justify increasing a business's size or domain breadth, acquiring other companies, merging or being acquired, automating processes, reorganising staff and teams, and increasing the fine-grained specialisation of the workforce and their machinery. Even outsourcing work might be rationalised with this phrase. However, are they telling their investors and staff the full story, or using a supposed "mathematical" economy of scale to hide a more complex decision involving trade-offs that may actually do as much or more harm than good? Would those in a position of authority, often too afraid to explore the nuanced trade-offs of any decision when analysed closely, use these oversimplifications as an excuse for any unwanted or unexpected consequences? Often, unfortunately, I suspect economies of scale are deliberately used to benefit the decision-makers without consequences.

Emergent properties are situations where the whole system is claimed to be greater than the sum of its parts. From my experience this idea appears most frequently in R&D, and in science. Examples include scientists discussing emergent properties of the brain, emergent properties of weather systems, of societies, of cities, of geological formations and recently so-called Artificial Intelligence. I would propose that in reality these properties were always predictable and one of three things have happened:

  • The author did not have a complete understanding of the system's components and their interactions
  • The author was not measuring the same qualities of the parts as they were the qualities of the system
  • The author wanted their topic to sound more mysterious and magical (very un-scientific)

To expand on this further, suppose we take a bicycle. Its frame, seat, handlebars, brakes, gears and wheels are indvidually much less useful for pedalling down the road than the whole bike, but of course it is their interactions in a specific order and assembly that make them useful. However, since we cannot ride just the brakes, nor just the handlebars, a basic mathematical equation of "how good each component is at being a bike" would never add up to their assembled bike. In this case, it is critical to realise that we have actually added "stuff" when assembling a bike: torque on the nuts and bolts and lubrication and component interactions, information and order in terms of the assembly process - this means the end result is absolutely the sum of its parts, it is just that the parts are more than its physical components sitting in little boxes.

Before I move on, I would like to point out that economies of scale and emergent properties are closely linked. In my mind, it makes more sense to imagine economies of scale as being an increase in magnitude, and emergent properties as an increase in complexity. Initially, I thought that maybe we should separate time-based economies from other magnitudes (i.e. think of it colloquially as "slow and steady wins the race"), rather than just making something larger it's making something faster. Ultimately however, time is a dimension and just like any other measurement can scale so I'm treating this as one and the same.

Summary table of concepts

Table of Concepts
Expression Property being scaled up Problem or error
Economies of scale Magnitude of (system) property Incomplete accounting of the effects of scaling up, or changes that are difficult to measure (quantitatively).
Emergent properties Complexity of system Incomplete understanding of increasing complexity, or incorrect accounting for the properties of the system and constituent parts.

In the follow sections, I aim to list as many examples of situations where either the increase in complexity or scale of a system has been incorrectly associated with emergent properties or economies of scale respectively. It may be overkill, but I feel it is important to highlight just how wide-ranging (and sometimes serious) these effects can be.

Examples

  1. Royal Mail Post Master's scandal
  2. The loss of Chadlington's local water supply

Royal Mail Post Master's Scandal

As an extremely short summary for those not already aware of the scandal, the British Post Office installed software called Horizon (written by ICL/Fujitsu) at all Post Office tills across their network of branches. This can be seen as a scaling up in control, standardisation and automatisation of what had once been a more independent, branch-specific and Post Master-controlled system.

The software was faulty, and through a mixture of what is generally seen as both institutional and moral failure by senior staff the Post Office prosecuted hundreds of Post Masters for theft, rather than identify the true culprit. The individual records of the Post Masters and their merits were seemingly ignored, and the aggression with which the Post Office wrongly pursued its own staff for theft became a topic of intense National anger once some very brave Post Office staff began to fight back. For a full description see the wikipedia article.

Water Networks

The first time I heard about Chadlington village's fight in the 1970s to keep its traditional spring water supply was on his Jeremy Clarkson's Amazon Farm series. It referenced an old piece of archived news footage from 1973 which explains the situation better than I could on this blog and it's only 6 minutes long.

In brief, the Water Company responsible in the area needed to supply some new houses up the hill from the village of Chadlington, which had for a long time used a local spring water source for all its houses and was famous for its water. Using the excuse of the new houses and its "institutional responsibility" for maintaining the same quality of water for everyone (even if worse...?), the water board ignored the villager's desire to keep drinking their local water and switched the supply one night by force. No petition from parliament nor protest from the village stopped it. In the video clip, the villagers complain about the taste of the new water supply and its effect on someone's fish tank.

This is an example of how as you increase networks, you eliminate (often to the point of ignoring basic local democracy) the individual desires and interests of people and their small settlements. Hundreds of years of tradition can be wiped out in a single night in the name of a national policy.

It might be worth adding that larger-scale water supply does change the water treatment process - another trade-off as we scale up our network. Manchester water always tasted highly chlorinated to me for example, unsurprising as it has to travel for 36 hours along 56 miles of pipes to reach the city from the Lake District. Higher levels of chlorination are necessary to keep the water pathogen-free for such a long journey. Contrast this with Macclesifled just South of the city which is next to its own reservoirs. The chlorination here is probably lower and I've personally found the water is much colder and less chemical-tasting when leaving the faucet.

Habitat fragmentation for the Cantabrian Capercaillie and Brown Bear

I won't describe in endless detail why a 20km squared plot of land is not equivalent to 2 x 10km squared plots of land (even if both halves are identical in features). But just as doubling something in size may lead to new problems, so will halving a plot of land.

One example is a complaint regarding the construction of a road in the North of Spain as described in the WWF here. They focus their attention on two species found in the mountains of Northern Spain which are emblematic of the area: the Cantabrian Brown Bear (el Oso Pardo, of which there are around 300) and the local subspecies of Cantabrian Capercaillie (el Orogallo, of which there are around 600). Both have/are decreasing dramatically in numbers and a key reason is thought to be habitat fragmentation. They need to roam freely over thousands of square kilometers of mountain terrain, but now transport routes amongst other human activities have divided the land up into smaller and smaller parcels.

It doesn't take much thought to see why this might happen, even if we ignore their "ideal" roaming areas. Two slices of land are almost never the same as they would have been if left whole, e.g.:

  • There might be a stream, forest, type of vegetation or soil difference between the two halves
  • Elevations and steepeness are unlikely to be the same on both sides
  • Different elevations may face different directions or have more or less shelter from weather systems
  • Roads can produce accidental separation of sexes and groups of the species and reduce genetic diversity
